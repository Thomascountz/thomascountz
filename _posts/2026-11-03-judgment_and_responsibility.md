Software is eating the world[^andreessen-2011] and the world is eating software; as career software programmers, we are privledged to have our programs affect this world in some way.

[^andreessen-2011]: https://a16z.com/why-software-is-eating-the-world/

Software doesn't exist only as the electrons pulsing through microscopic labyrinths painstakingly grown from rare earths, it exists within the way it influences the milleau around it. A software program's purpose is to _do_ something, and it does it's _something_ by providing certain useful affordances within a set of specific constraints.

If you program RTOS firmware, your software may be used to make road traffic safer by adjusting the timing of light signals. If you write mobile applications, your software might be helping travellers navigate around a foreign country by providing translations and maps. If you program data analysis pipelines, your software could very well be used to advance science by processing terabytes of astronomical data each day. 

Regardless of the kinds of software you build, if you program professionaly, your software will hopefully be purposefully useful. Therefore, before any software should come to exist, and before decisions can be made about it, both its purpose and its impacts must be understood.

Having the role of a professional programmer requires that you to take responsibility for your understanding. Producing code is sometimes part of your work, and so too is understanding what software is, how it does what it does, and why it should exist. This responsibility begins before implementation and continues on as long as the software is run. 

Running software changes the milleau around it, i.e. it influences and iteracts with the environment across different dimensions.  

If you do not take up the reponsibility of understanding the breath of impacts your software has or will have, you cannot make decisions; you cannot be a professional programmer. 

Not everyone who works on software is functioning in a professional capacity. Sometimes this is related to an individual, sometimes to a role or project. What should be clear by now is that professional programming requires decisions, decisions require responsibility, and responsibility requires understanding.

Holding onto this belief requires a high degree of intellectual honesty and curiousity. Without it, how would one expect a programmer to successfully identify, evaluate, and _make decisions_ about the constrains which influence of their program's usefulness, value, and outcomes? 

In the formal engineering disciplines, constraints are often physical, fixed, or unignorable. For the software programmer, tradeoffs are significantly more fluid and dynamic—that's part of what makes software so powerful, and part of why so many decisions need to be made.

If you do not make these decisions yourself, it does not mean they will go unmade; it means only that you will not know they have been made.

If it becomes systemically convenient to ignore—if external incentives dissuade understanding—professionalism is the backstop.

Business realities belong in this practice, which includes the work of operating and maintaining the software. Software programs should be honorably valuable to the creators and to the systems in which they're used. However, there is an imbalance: the economy of software is exactly the fact that both the positive and negative impacts of our decisions out-scale the cost to build and deliver it. Therefore, when understanding impact and value, it's often advisable to spend most of the effort on the end-system.
