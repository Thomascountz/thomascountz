It's nearing the end of 2026 and I've realized that AI has changed very little about my job as a software developer. 

That's not entirely true. AI has certainly changed the way I relate to my peers in the tech industry. And, it has forced me to reckon with things that I've taken for granted—things about developing software that haven't changed and that I thought every programmer knew.

Here's an example: technical debt.

Tech debt is a euphemism. From the name, it sounds like it's about borrowing time. The story goes that you cut a few corners and call a project done, and then you pay back that time you saved, at some later point. You pay back the "tech debt" by spending time today, doing what you would have done yesterday, if you had more time.

But obviously (or so I thought), that's a just a convenient shorthand; a kind of mental model.

Taking on technical debt means we're not going to spend time doing the thing that was planned, because it takes longer than we want. We don't "save time", as it were; we ship ealier.

And we don't "pay" for this with our time at some later point. We pay for it by giving up one or some of the following: completeness, correctness, performance, reliability, usability, maintainability, security, or safety. We give up those things, or else we give up _the ability to know_ whether or not we're giving up those things. In either case, they incur additional costs.

Taking on technical debt means asking whether the value of users having an indebted or compromised version of the software one week sooner, is worth more than the value of customers having a more correct version of the product one week later, minus the costs of one week of engineering?

I have no qualms with accepting tech debt. I have qualms with software developers being comfortable accepting only the mythical version of it. 

A large part of what I believe software engineers are responsible for is guiding product management on these tradeoffs. I don't know your situation, so I can't advise you on what decisions to make, but I do know that you can't make responsible decisions without intellectual integrity and honesty.

It's the end of 2026 and AI has not changed this equation. If anything, it's made it more imporant to understand. "The agents will fix it" is a religious belief; it is not a real engineering strategy yet. In my line of work, I believe we can do better than that.
